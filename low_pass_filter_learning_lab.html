1. Introduction and prerequisites

A low-pass filter (LPF) is a circuit that allows signals below a selected frequency to pass with relatively little attenuation while reducing signals at higher frequencies. The transition is not an abrupt wall: a practical filter rolls off gradually.

Simple memory aid: Low frequencies get through; high frequencies are reduced. The cut-off frequency marks the point where the output magnitude has fallen to about 70.7% of its passband value (−3 dB).

Why it matters

LPFs remove high-frequency noise, smooth sensor signals, limit bandwidth before sampling, and shape audio or control signals. Active LPFs use an op-amp with resistors and capacitors, allowing gain and buffering without an inductor.

Prerequisites

Ohm’s law, capacitor impedance, op-amp negative feedback, gain, logarithms and decibels.

Important terms

Passband, stopband, cut-off frequency \(f_c\), bandwidth, roll-off, gain, phase shift and Bode plot.

Types

Passive RC LPF; active first-order LPF; higher-order LPF; Butterworth, Chebyshev and other response families.

3. Working principle: step by step

  1. Low frequency: A capacitor’s reactance \(X_C=1/(2\pi fC)\) is large. Little signal current flows through C, so the RC node is close to the input voltage.
  2. As frequency rises: Capacitive reactance falls. More of the signal is diverted through the capacitor to ground.
  3. At the cut-off frequency: For a first-order RC network, the magnitude is \(1/\sqrt{2}\) of its low-frequency value.
  4. Above cut-off: The capacitor increasingly shunts the signal; the output magnitude decreases at approximately 20 dB per decade for a first-order filter.
  5. Op-amp action: Negative feedback provides a nearly constant passband gain \(A_F\), within the op-amp’s bandwidth, slew-rate and output-swing limits.

DC and AC behaviour

DC / very low frequency

At DC, an ideal capacitor is open-circuit. The RC node follows the input; the op-amp output is approximately \(A_F V_{in}\), provided it remains in its linear range.

AC / high frequency

The capacitor impedance decreases with frequency, reducing the RC node voltage. Real op-amp limitations can add further attenuation and phase shift.

Assumptions

  • First-order RC network with ideal components and a high-input-impedance op-amp input.
  • The op-amp operates linearly with negative feedback and adequate supply voltage.
  • The basic cut-off formula describes the RC pole; real op-amp bandwidth may affect the measured response.

Advantages and limitations

Simple, compact, inexpensive; active version can provide gain and buffering; no inductor required.First-order roll-off is only 20 dB/decade; op-amp noise, bandwidth, slew rate and supply rails matter; component tolerances shift \(f_c\).

4. Equations and derivation

Capacitive reactance

\(X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi fC}\)

\(X_C\) is in ohms (Ω), \(f\) in hertz (Hz), and \(C\) in farads (F). As \(f\) increases, \(X_C\) decreases.

Transfer function of the RC low-pass network

For a series resistor R and capacitor C to ground, take the output across C. The capacitor impedance is \(Z_C=1/(sC)\). Using the voltage divider:

\(H(s)=\frac{V_o(s)}{V_i(s)}=\frac{Z_C}{R+Z_C}=\frac{1}{1+sRC}\)

For \(s=j\omega\), the magnitude and phase are:

\(|H(j\omega)|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}}\),   \(\angle H=-\tan^{-1}(\omega RC)\)

At \(\omega_c RC=1\), therefore:

\(\omega_c=\frac{1}{RC}\),   \(f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi RC}\)

At \(f=f_c\), \(|H|=1/\sqrt{2}\approx0.707\), and phase is −45° for the passive first-order RC network.

Active filter gain

\(A_F=1+\frac{R_F}{R_G}\)

For a buffered active first-order design, the ideal overall transfer function is \(H(s)=A_F/(1+sRC)\), assuming the op-amp has sufficient bandwidth. Its passband gain is \(A_F\); at the pole, the magnitude is \(A_F/\sqrt{2}\).

Decibels and roll-off

\(G_{dB}=20\log_{10}\left|\frac{V_o}{V_i}\right|\)

At cut-off relative to passband, \(20\log_{10}(0.707)\approx -3.01\) dB. A first-order low-pass has an asymptotic high-frequency slope of −20 dB/decade (about −6 dB/octave).

Limiting cases

  • \(f\to0\): \(|H|\to1\) for the passive network, or \(A_F\) for the active stage.
  • \(f=f_c\): magnitude is passband magnitude divided by \(\sqrt{2}\).
  • \(f\gg f_c\): magnitude decreases approximately in proportion to \(1/f\).

5. Solved numerical examples

Example 1 — Find cut-off frequency (Easy)

Given: \(R=10\,k\Omega\), \(C=10\,nF\). Find: \(f_c\).

Formula: \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\).

Substitution: \(RC=(10,000)(10\times10^{-9})=100\times10^{-6}\,s\).

\(f_c=\frac{1}{2\pi(100\times10^{-6})}\approx1591.5\,Hz\)

Answer: \(f_c\approx1.59\,kHz\). Frequencies well below this are passed with little attenuation.

Example 2 — Output at cut-off (Moderate)

Given: active filter passband gain \(A_F=3\), input sine amplitude \(V_i=0.20\,V\), frequency equals \(f_c\). Find: output amplitude.

At cut-off, output magnitude is \(A_F V_i/\sqrt{2}\).

\(V_o=\frac{3(0.20)}{\sqrt{2}}=\frac{0.60}{1.414}\approx0.424\,V\)

Answer: approximately 0.424 V peak, assuming linear operation and negligible extra op-amp attenuation.

Example 3 — Design for a target cut-off (GATE-style)

Given: \(f_c=1\,kHz\), choose \(C=15\,nF\). Find: the required R.

Rearrange \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\): \(R=1/(2\pi f_c C)\).

\(R=\frac{1}{2\pi(1000)(15\times10^{-9})}\approx10.61\,k\Omega\)

Answer: \(R\approx10.6\,k\Omega\). A standard 10.5 kΩ or 11 kΩ part gives a nearby cut-off; recalculate using the chosen real value.

Fast shortcut: If R is in kΩ and C in nF, \(f_c\text{ (kHz)}\approx159.15/[R\text{ (kΩ)}\,C\text{ (nF)}]\).

Build and test in Multisim

  1. Place an AC voltage source, resistor R, capacitor C, ground, an op-amp, feedback resistors and suitable supply rails.
  2. Wire R in series from the source to the non-inverting input node; connect C from that node to ground.
  3. Wire the non-inverting amplifier feedback divider: \(R_F\) from output to inverting input and \(R_G\) from inverting input to ground.
  4. Set R and C. For 10 kΩ and 10 nF, expect the RC pole near 1.59 kHz.
  5. Use AC Analysis/Bode Plotter to sweep frequency across at least two decades below and above \(f_c\).
  6. Measure passband gain, find the frequency where gain is 3 dB below passband, and compare it with \(1/(2\pi RC)\).
  7. For transient analysis, compare input and output sine waves at a low frequency, at \(f_c\), and well above \(f_c\).

What to check if the simulation differs

  • Confirm the capacitor is connected to ground at the intended RC node.
  • Check op-amp supply pins, polarity, feedback wiring and output saturation.
  • Use the AC-analysis magnitude relative to passband gain, not necessarily relative to 0 dB when \(A_F\ne1\).
  • Real op-amp gain-bandwidth, source resistance, load and component tolerances can shift the observed response.

8. Core electronics interview questions

1. What is a low-pass filter?

A frequency-selective circuit that passes low-frequency components and attenuates higher-frequency components beyond its cut-off region.

2. Define the cut-off frequency of a first-order LPF.

The frequency at which magnitude falls to \(1/\sqrt2\) (about 70.7%) of its passband value, corresponding to approximately −3 dB.

3. Derive the RC low-pass cut-off frequency.

For \(H(s)=1/(1+sRC)\), set \(|H|=1/\sqrt{1+(\omega RC)^2}=1/\sqrt2\). This gives \(\omega RC=1\), so \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\).

4. Why use an active LPF instead of a passive one?

An active LPF can provide voltage gain and buffering, reducing loading between stages. It needs power and is limited by op-amp bandwidth, noise, slew rate and output swing.

5. What is the roll-off of a first-order LPF?

Its asymptotic magnitude slope is −20 dB/decade (about −6 dB/octave) above the pole.

6. What is the phase shift at cut-off for a first-order RC LPF?

The RC network has a phase shift of −45° at \(f_c\). Additional op-amp dynamics can add phase shift.

7. How would you verify the cut-off in Multisim?

Run AC analysis, measure the passband gain, then locate the frequency where magnitude is 3 dB lower. Compare against \(1/(2\pi RC)\).

8. The measured cut-off is too low. What would you inspect?

Check whether R or C is larger than intended, whether source/load resistance changes the effective network, and whether the op-amp’s bandwidth or setup adds a pole.

9. Why can’t an op-amp output any amplitude or frequency?

Output swing is limited by supply rails, and slew rate and gain-bandwidth limit large or high-frequency signals. Exceeding limits causes distortion or attenuation.

10. Give an industry use of an LPF.

Anti-alias conditioning before an ADC, sensor-noise reduction, audio tone shaping, power-supply ripple filtering, and smoothing in control systems. Requirements depend on noise spectrum, signal bandwidth and ADC sampling rate.

11. How does a second-order LPF differ from a first-order one?

A second-order response can roll off at approximately −40 dB/decade after its poles; its damping/Q affects peaking and transient response.

12. What practical component tolerances matter?

R and C tolerances affect \(f_c\); op-amp input bias/offset, noise, finite bandwidth, loading and temperature drift also matter.

Industry perspective

Analog design and hardware-validation engineers use LPFs while conditioning sensor outputs, protecting ADC input bandwidth, reducing out-of-band interference, and validating signal-chain performance. A good design starts from the wanted signal bandwidth and noise requirements, then checks tolerance, loading, op-amp limits and measured frequency response.

High-priority exam points

  • Remember \(f_c=1/(2\pi RC)\); larger R or C means lower cut-off frequency.
  • At cut-off, output magnitude is 0.707 of passband magnitude, not necessarily 0.707 V.
  • For the basic RC LPF, phase is −45° at cut-off and tends toward −90° at very high frequency.
  • Use the −3 dB point relative to passband gain for an active filter with gain greater than one.
  • Real op-amp bandwidth and loading may change measured behaviour.

Common mistakes and troubleshooting

  • Forgetting to convert nF to F or kΩ to Ω.
  • Confusing low-pass and high-pass capacitor placement.
  • Calling the cut-off a brick-wall boundary.
  • Assuming the op-amp can produce unlimited output swing or bandwidth.
  • Ignoring loading, grounding, supply rails or component tolerance in simulation.
